# Voice & Style Guide

A reference for drafting new content (posts, replies, about-page copy) in Nathan's voice. Built from a read-through and word-frequency analysis of the 431 blog posts dated before 2026 in `src/content/posts/` — the recovered 2002–2013 archive plus the handful of 2014 stragglers. The 2026 "relaunch" posts were excluded from the corpus since the goal is to capture the original, pre-hiatus voice.

This is descriptive, not prescriptive: it documents what the writing actually does, so future drafts can match it rather than default to generic "AI blog voice."

## The short version

Write like you're telling a mate what happened, not like you're presenting to a client. Short paragraphs, first person, British spelling, an opinion stated plainly and then hedged half a beat later ("I think", "I guess", "perhaps"), a joke or a jab at yourself if one's available, and a full stop before the post overstays its welcome. Median post length is **107 words**. Most posts don't need headers, bullet lists, or a big structural apparatus — they need three or four sentences that get to the point.

## Voice & persona

- **First person, present-tense opinions.** Posts are written in the moment — "I've been giving up Coffee for 2 weeks now", "I think I have perhaps stumbled across ExpressionEngine's best feature yet." Not retrospective case studies; live dispatches.
- **Enthusiast-practitioner, not authority.** The default stance is "here's a thing I found / a thing that annoyed me / a thing I made," never "here is the definitive guide to X." Even genuinely useful how-tos are framed as one person's workaround, not doctrine.
- **Self-deprecating before self-important.** "Maybe I've been doing something wrong, maybe I'm just thick but..." (*when-upgrades-turn-bad*). Confidence is expressed about the *situation* ("clients hate scrolling," full stop) far more than about the writer.
- **Direct address to the reader.** Posts frequently turn and ask the reader something — "do you prefer paging or scrolling…? Me.., I think scrolling is just fine" — rather than lecturing outward.
- **Dry, understated humour over jokes-that-announce-themselves.** The comic beat is usually a deadpan aside or a single wry line at the end of a paragraph, not a bit that's built up to.
- **British English throughout.** `whilst`, `amongst`, `favourite`, `colour`, `realise`, `centre`, `behaviour`. Never Americanise these in edits or new drafts.

## Tone by context

The voice flexes by post type but the underlying register — plain-spoken, contraction-heavy, opinionated — stays constant.

| Context | Tone |
|---|---|
| Personal/family updates | Warm, brief, matter-of-fact about big news ("So after a quick call to NHS Direct we rushed over to the Maternity unit... it's a girl!") |
| Product/tool gripes | Exasperated but funny — frustration rendered as comedy, not a rant for its own sake ("nothing, nada, zip") |
| Technical how-tos | Practical and unfussy, first person throughout ("Get the low-down on how to use the feature right here. In the Manual no less, I know… **RTFM**!") |
| Interviews ("10 questions for...") | Conversational Q&A format, **Nathan:** / **Name:** bold labels, section headers used as playful chapter titles ("Ch ch ch ch changes") |
| Link/curation posts | Terse — sometimes a single sentence plus a screenshot, no padding added just to hit a word count |
| Business/freelance reflections | Slightly more measured, but still first person and still ends with a personal aside rather than a tidy conclusion |

## Structure & form

- **Short by default.** Median 107 words, mean ~160. Plenty of legitimate posts are one or two sentences (a link + one line of commentary is a complete post, not a stub).
- **Headers are the exception, not the rule.** Only ~5% of posts use `##`/`###` — reserve them for genuinely multi-part pieces (interviews, structured round-ups), not for pieces this guide is used on by default.
- **Bullet lists are occasional, not structural scaffolding.** ~11% of posts use one, usually for a short list of links or options — never turn a 100-word post into a bulleted outline.
- **Bold is used sparingly and functionally** — most often for a `**Update:**` prefix on a later addendum, or to land a single emphatic word (`**RTFM**`), not for scattering emphasis through a paragraph.
- **Posts often open in the middle of the situation**, not with throat-clearing preamble. Compare the actual opener "We had a bit of a baby related scare this morning" against a generic "In this post I want to talk about..." — always prefer the former shape.
- **Endings are abrupt, not summarised.** Posts stop when the thought is done. Avoid adding a wrap-up paragraph that restates what was just said — the corpus essentially never does this.

## Punctuation & mechanics

Measured across the corpus (431 posts, ~61,000 words):

- **Contractions are constant** (~1,200 instances — roughly 1 every 50 words). `don't`, `it's`, `I've`, `we're`. Never write around a contraction to sound more formal.
- **Curly/smart apostrophes and quotes** are the house style throughout (`'`/`'`, `'`/`'`), a holdover from the original blogging platform.
- **Ellipses are a real tic** (259 instances) — used mid-sentence for a trailing-off or a comic beat: "nothing, nada, zip", "way back in 1999... and then I moved to Iceland". Use `…` or `...` to land a wry pause, not just at the end of a list.
- **Em dashes are rare** (~22 instances across the whole corpus). This is *not* an em-dash-heavy voice — don't reach for one the way default AI prose does. Prefer a comma, a full stop, or an ellipsis instead.
- **Parentheses are frequent** (1,436 instances) — used constantly for a quick aside, caveat, or self-correction dropped inline: "(It was a hard day at work, I was weak)", "(like myself)". This is one of the strongest structural habits in the corpus — lean on it.
- **Exclamation marks (352) and question marks (378) both show up often** — genuine enthusiasm and genuine rhetorical questions, not just for emphasis. Don't be afraid of either, but don't stack them.
- **Emoticons, not emoji**: `:)` appears 111 times, `:(` occasionally. Text-based smileys, in keeping with the era — use `:)` rather than 🙂 if a light sign-off is warranted (only for genuinely casual/personal posts, not technical ones).
- **Semicolons are used sparingly** (98 instances) — a comma or a full stop is preferred in most cases where a semicolon might otherwise go.

## Recurring phrases & rhetorical habits

These aren't clichés to purge — they're load-bearing verbal tics that make a draft sound like Nathan rather than like a style-matched impostor:

- **Hedges dropped mid-thought**: "I think" (54×), "I guess" (28×), "perhaps", "kind of" (15×), "sort of", "rather". An opinion is stated and then immediately softened — this is a feature, not wishy-washiness.
- **Conversational fillers**: "you know" (25×), "obviously", "actually", "basically" — used the way they'd land in speech.
- **Sentence-opening connectors**: "So..." and "Anyway..." both used to pivot or wrap up a thought casually, instead of a formal transition.
- **Direct rhetorical questions to the reader**, often self-answered: "...don't you think?", "...right?"
- **"Right", "sure", "fair enough"** as small confirming beats.
- **Understated intensifiers rather than superlatives**: "pretty" (as in "pretty easy"), "quite", "fairly" — used more than maximal words like "incredibly" or "extremely", which barely appear at all.

## Lexicon

Words genuinely reached for across the corpus, grouped by function. Treat this as a palette, not a checklist — don't cram several into one sentence.

### Approval / enthusiasm
`great`, `fantastic`, `nice`, `excellent`, `brilliant`, `lovely`, `gorgeous`, `stunning`, `superb`, `solid`, `neat`, `handy`, `nifty`, `shiny`, `interesting`, `worth [a read/a look]`

### Mild frustration / gripe
`daft`, `ghastly`, `rubbish`, `annoying`, `frustrating`, `painful`, `clunky`, `fiddly`, `flimsy`, `broken`, `a nightmare`, `suffice to say`, `leaves something to be desired`

### Hedging / qualifying
`perhaps`, `maybe`, `I think`, `I guess`, `kind of`, `sort of`, `rather`, `apparently`, `seemingly`, `I'm assuming`

### Emphasis / degree (understated register — prefer these over maximal superlatives)
`pretty` (pretty easy / pretty good), `quite`, `fairly`, `simply`, `right` (right here / right now), `totally`, `absolute [must-read]`, `so often`

### Directness / conclusion beats
`suffice to say`, `so there you go`, `fair enough`, `to be on the safe side`, `good stuff`

### British-specific vocabulary (always use these forms)
`whilst`, `amongst`, `favourite`, `colour`, `realise`, `centre`, `behaviour`, `organise`, `travelling`

### Signature small words (unusually frequent — don't be afraid to reuse)
`stuff`, `bit` ("a bit of a scare", "a little bit"), `thing`/`things`, `way` ("way back in 1999"), `proper`/`properly`

## Anti-patterns — what this voice is *not*

Avoid these even though they're common in generic AI-drafted blog prose — none of them appear in the actual corpus at meaningful rates:

- **Heavy em-dash use.** The corpus uses ~22 em dashes across 431 posts. Default AI prose over-uses them; this voice doesn't. Reach for a comma, full stop, or parenthesis instead.
- **Tidy summarising conclusions.** Don't add a closing paragraph that restates the post's point. Posts just stop.
- **Formal transitions** ("Furthermore," "In conclusion," "It's worth noting that..."). Use "So," "Anyway," or nothing at all.
- **Corporate/marketing adjectives** ("robust," "seamless," "cutting-edge," "leverage," "game-changing"). None of these appear in the corpus. Prefer the plainer, more physical words in the lexicon above.
- **American spelling.** Always British.
- **Padding a short post to hit a word count.** A single sentence plus a link or screenshot is a legitimate, complete post in this voice.
- **Excessive headers/bullets on a short piece.** Reserve structure for posts that are genuinely multi-part (interviews, round-ups).
- **Maximal superlatives** ("incredible," "unbelievable," "revolutionary"). The corpus reaches for "pretty," "quite," and "great" far more often than anything maximal.

## Quick checklist for drafting

1. First person, present-moment framing — not a retrospective case study.
2. Keep it short. If it's under 150 words, that's normal, not a rough draft.
3. Open in the middle of the situation, not with throat-clearing.
4. Drop in at least one hedge ("I think", "perhaps", "I guess") near an opinion.
5. Use a parenthetical aside somewhere if a natural one presents itself.
6. British spelling throughout.
7. Contractions everywhere it's natural to speak them.
8. End when the thought is done — no summary paragraph.
9. No em dashes unless one is truly earned; reach for a comma or ellipsis instead.
10. Skip headers and bullet lists unless the piece genuinely has multiple parts.

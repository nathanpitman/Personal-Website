I was tidying through some very old bookmarks recently and stumbled back onto [head-space.org](https://head-space.org/) — and with it, a name I hadn't thought about in twenty-odd years: **.matrix**.

I'd have guessed I got involved sometime around 2000–2002. I was wrong by a couple of years. Between the Wayback Machine and my own original source files (still sitting, of all places, in an old backup folder), I can now date this precisely: the `index.html` for .matrix carries a creation date of **21st January 1998**, and the earliest surviving build file — `MATRIX1.fla` — is timestamped **24th February 1998**. That makes .matrix one of Head-Space's earlier chapters, not a late addition to something already decorated with a Cannes Lion — the award came a year later, in 1999.

Before I get into what .matrix actually was, it's worth explaining what Head-Space itself turned out to be — because at the time, I don't think any of us fully knew.

## What Head-Space actually was

Head-Space started life in 1997, out of a Brixton agency called [Head New Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_New_Media), founded by **[Felix Velarde](https://www.head-space.org/felix/)** and **[Jason Michael Holland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jason_Michael_Holland)**. Head New Media grew fast — by 1998 it was the UK digital arm of Lowe Worldwide (part of the Interpublic network), spun up its own interactive TV agency, Head End, and was building award-winning work for the BBC, Unilever, Tesco, GM and PwC. The Snickers MegaBite site picked up global ad awards in '97–98. This was serious, commercially successful agency work.

Head-Space was the opposite of that on purpose. It was the agency's 20%-time project — a non-commercial space, deliberately outside client billing, where staff and outside contributors could make whatever they wanted. No briefs, no deliverables, no rules beyond "nothing commercial." Felix and Jason described it, at various points, as a refuge from the restrictions of client work.

And it outgrew everything around it. Head-Space won a **Cannes Lion in 1999** for Best Online Community, co-credited to Velarde and Holland. It's credited with incubating other sites, including [Urban75](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban75). [Management Today](https://www.managementtoday.co.uk/10-websites-changed-world/article/1102817) later named it one of the **"Ten Websites That Changed the World,"** calling it "the blueprint for intranets everywhere — and YouTube." It's also been part of the touring **[Digital Archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_Archaeology_(exhibition))** exhibition since 2010, curated by design historian Jim Boulton, which recognises it in similar terms — as an early, germinal precursor to YouTube. Odd thing to learn about something you once put your own work into.

Head New Media itself didn't survive — it was [liquidated in 2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_New_Media) when its partnership with Lowe came apart. Felix went on to run Underwired, which appears to be who's kept head-space.org alive ever since; the site relaunched as "v5" in 2002, with Felix and Jason (posting as FV and JH) still updating it with news well into 2004.

## Where .matrix fits in

The site organises contributors into individually-run "chapters" — Felix's own was called Technohippy, Jason's was Deadsheep, and there were a dozen or so others: Fractal Billy, tØpØgraphic, Luminus Labs, Worldmaker, and more. Mine was **.matrix**. (My own earliest source files list other names credited alongside me at the time too — Paul Maguire, Thomas Betts, John Lundberg, Ben Evans, and someone recorded only as "Kitty" — none of whom appear on the site's current roster, wherever they ended up.)

In my own words, .matrix was "a collection of my own digital wanderings" — Macromedia Shockwave content, Flash experiments, and digital art, built as somewhere to share what I was producing while studying product design at **Berkshire College of Art and Design, 1996–98**. A leftover coursework page from the same folder lists what I was actually designing back then: an Apple ePad concept, a multimedia kiosk, a mixer, a shopping trolley, a beer can.

The build history, recovered from my own source files, turns out to be far more granular than I remembered. .matrix went through **at least nine numbered iterations** — `MATRIX1.fla` through `MATRIX9.fla` — built roughly monthly across 1998, from that February start through to early November. Partway through, in July 1998, I built a standalone Windows screensaver called **"the_n"** for it — modelled in 3D Studio Max, composited in Adobe Premiere, assembled as a film loop in Director 6.5, with title text done in Flash 3, then converted to a screensaver file with a tool called Cinemac. Two of those early builds link out to downloadable packages named `wireframe.zip` and `liquid.zip`, and point back to `head-space.com`.

There's a second act, too: a redesign attempt called **"rev2000"** appears in the files from January through November 2000, before .matrix was rebuilt one more time in the summer of 2001 — the version that's the one still live on the site today. That final build is a menu of **25 numbered slots**, each meant to hold its own sub-project, loaded dynamically when clicked. Only three ever got finished and wired in: **`dotxt`** ("a small flash application for keeping track of daily tasks"), **`elasticity`** ("a short experiment in elasticity and simulated shadows"), and **`mmextensions`** (a set of Dreamweaver extensions I'd written that Macromedia had approved). Twenty-two slots sat empty. Which feels, in hindsight, like a fairly honest snapshot of any personal project of this scale — more ambition than follow-through, and nothing wrong with that.

I also contributed to Head-Space's shared "Free Stuff" wallpaper gallery, separately from my own chapter — six numbered images (016, 017, 021, 027, 028, 031) sitting alongside desktop backgrounds from other contributors.

As best I can remember it, there was no introduction, no one spotting my college work and inviting me in. I found the site myself — I don't remember how, probably just following links the way you did back then — and applied for a chapter through the site itself, the same as (I assume) everyone else on that contributors list.

## Tracking down the others

The site's [people involved](https://head-space.org/peopleinvolved.php) page lists the other chapter-runners from the same era, and I wanted to know where they all ended up. Results were mixed. **Jason Kitcat** — listed in my own site's keywords alongside me — I'm reasonably confident I've traced; he went on to a public career in local politics and open-source civic tech. A couple of others turn up plausible matches under the same names doing design or development work today, but nothing solid enough to put a name to a person with confidence. The rest — Huw Nicholls, Micah Boswell, Kim Granlund, Stéphane Poirier, Masatomo Ueda, Ruth Brecher, Billy Rood, among others — I couldn't place at all. If any of that's you, or you know where they landed, get in touch.

## Why this one's stuck with me

Server space wasn't cheap in 1998. Neither was a lot of the software I was using to make this stuff. Having somewhere to put digital experimentation online, for free, no brief, no client, straight out of college — that mattered more than I probably clocked at the time. I don't think it's a coincidence that a chapter built out of Flash tests and a homemade screensaver turned into a twenty-five-year interest in the web and in making things online for a living. .matrix wasn't going anywhere in particular. It didn't need to. That was rather the point of Head-Space.

If you were part of Head-Space too, I'd love to hear from you.
